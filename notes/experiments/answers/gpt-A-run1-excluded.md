P-1. Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

A traffic jam is a real phenomenon, but it is not an additional object alongside the cars. It is a pattern of constrained movement arising from interactions among vehicles, drivers, and road conditions.

The cars participating in a jam can change while the jam persists. It can also propagate backward along a highway even though every car moves forward. Its movement is therefore different from the movement of its constituent vehicles.

A traffic jam can be identified, tracked, measured, and causally investigated. Calling it a pattern rather than an object does not make it less real.

P-2. If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

It can be. If the planks are replaced gradually while the ship continues operating, there is a reasonable basis for regarding it as the same ship. Its material composition changes, but its history and functional organization remain continuous.

The difficulty becomes apparent if someone collects the discarded planks and reconstructs another ship. Which one is the original? Continuity of operation favors the maintained ship; continuity of original material favors the reconstruction.

There is no universally compulsory answer independent of what we mean by the same ship. Identity depends on the criteria appropriate to what we are tracking. Those criteria need not require material permanence, but they must address cases of branching and competing continuities.

P-3. Is a glider in Conway’s Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

A glider is real as a dynamically persistent pattern in the Game of Life. It is not a separate physical ingredient of the grid, but neither is it simply a fixed collection of live cells.

Each generation consists entirely of cells changing states according to the local update rule. Yet across four generations, the glider reproduces its configuration displaced by one cell diagonally. The pattern travels even though the cells themselves occupy fixed grid positions.

The cellular and glider descriptions track different kinds of continuity. A cell retains its location while changing state; a glider retains its recognizable organization while changing location and cellular realization. Both descriptions refer to the same evolving grid, but identify different things within it.

P-4. Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

Usually, yes. A river remains identifiable despite continuously exchanging water, transporting sediment, eroding its banks, and sometimes changing its course.

What persists is not a particular collection of water molecules but a connected hydrological process: water flowing through a drainage system, sustained by rainfall, groundwater, terrain, and other conditions.

However, river identity becomes less definite when channels divide, merge, dry up, or are artificially redirected. Whether a river remains the same depends on which continuity matters: its course, its watershed, its historical identity, or its ongoing flow. Ordinary cases are straightforward; exceptional cases expose the limits of our criteria.

U-1. Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

Not quite. Most money today is not paper at all; it consists of bank deposits and other recorded claims. Even physical currency is more than its material substrate.

Money is a social and institutional arrangement. Its value and function depend on practices involving exchange, accounting, taxation, debt settlement, legal authority, and collective expectations. A banknote remains physically unchanged when its purchasing power collapses.

The fact that money depends on collective recognition does not make it imaginary. Debts, prices, and payments have real consequences. Money is real as an institutional phenomenon, although its existence and effectiveness depend on a functioning social system.

U-2. Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

No. The conclusion does not follow from the observation alone. Failing to find the self as one item among bodily processes, thoughts, and sensations establishes only that the self has not been identified as such an item.

Consider a traffic jam. Searching among the cars will not reveal an additional object called the jam. But the jam is not therefore imaginary. It is identifiable through the changing organization of traffic.

Similarly, the self might be understood as an ongoing organization of bodily, psychological, and interpersonal processes rather than a separate internal object. This does not establish that such an organization constitutes a self, or explain subjective experience. It does show why failing to locate an independent, unchanging observer is insufficient to establish that no self exists.

U-3. A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

Not necessarily. The game world and the graphics card belong to different levels of description. The character inhabits a simulated environment defined by the game’s rules; the graphics card is part of the physical machinery implementing that environment.

A mountain in the game is not a physical mountain. Nevertheless, it can be a real feature of the simulated world, with a definite position, structure, and consequences for the character’s actions. Its existence as a simulated mountain does not require it to resemble its underlying hardware.

There is an important asymmetry: the game depends on physical implementation, while the graphics card does not depend on that particular game. But dependence is not the same as unreality. The game world is real as a simulation, not as an independently existing physical environment.

U-4. Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

No. Explaining temperature through microscopic physics does not eliminate temperature.

Temperature is a thermodynamic property of a system. In appropriate conditions it is related to the average kinetic energy of particles, but that account is incomplete: molecular interactions, quantum statistics, and the system’s thermodynamic state also matter. More generally, temperature is defined through thermal equilibrium and thermodynamic relations.

Temperature can be measured, predicts heat transfer, and figures in laws that apply to macroscopic systems. Its microscopic explanation tells us how this property is realized, not that the property is fictional. The mistake is to assume that a phenomenon becomes unreal once its physical basis is understood.

N-1. Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

In an ordinary loose pile, there is little reason to posit a new entity with an identity independent of the bricks and their arrangement.

The pile has collective properties: height, shape, weight distribution, and stability. These properties are not properties of any individual brick, but they follow from the bricks and their spatial relationships.

We can meaningfully call the pile a thing, but its boundaries and persistence conditions are often conventional. Remove a brick, and it probably remains the same pile. Scatter the bricks, and the pile disappears. This does not require a distinct organizing process that persists independently of its components.

N-2. Is a car engine made of its parts?

Yes, but an engine is not merely an unordered collection of those parts. Their arrangement, connections, and coordinated operation are essential to its being an engine.

A disassembled engine may contain every original component without being capable of running. Assembly establishes mechanical relationships through which combustion, force transmission, lubrication, and cooling can occur. The resulting system has capacities that isolated components lack.

These capacities do not require an extra substance beyond the components and their interactions. An engine is a structured physical whole. Its organization matters, but its operation is explained through the behavior and relationships of its constituent parts.

N-3. Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

A block is a stable configuration of four live cells arranged in a square. Under Conway’s rules, none of those cells changes state, and the surrounding dead cells remain dead.

Unlike a glider, the block does not migrate across the grid or reproduce its organization through successive sets of live cells. Its persistence coincides with the continued existence of the same four live cells in the same positions.

The block can still be described as a pattern, and that description is useful. But its identity does not clearly separate from the identity and arrangement of its constituent cells. It is a configuration that remains unchanged, rather than a moving pattern whose continuity crosses changing cellular realizations.

N-4. Is a sentence made of words?

A sentence is composed of words organized according to grammatical and semantic relationships. The words alone, considered as an unordered collection, do not determine the sentence.

For example, “The dog chased the cat” and “The cat chased the dog” contain the same words but express different propositions. Word order and grammatical structure determine who does what to whom.

A sentence can also remain the same sentence when spoken by different people, printed on different pages, or encoded in different digital files. Its linguistic identity is therefore distinct from the identity of any particular physical inscription. Still, a sentence is not an independent substance added to its words: it is a structured linguistic unit that can have multiple realizations.
