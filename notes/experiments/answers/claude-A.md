Q-1. Real. The glider is a pattern with its own identity conditions: a five-cell shape that cycles through four phases and moves one cell diagonally every four generations. It has a position, a velocity and a heading, and it has effects. Gliders collide, annihilate, get eaten, and carry signals in Life computers built from glider guns.

The decisive fact is that no cell travels with it. Cells stay put and switch, while the glider moves across them. So "just cells switching" and "a glider moving" both describe the same events correctly. Only the glider description compresses them and predicts what happens next at the level where the interactions occur.

Q-2. Materially, yes. Every atom in the engine belongs to some part. The engine is those parts in a specific working arrangement, though. Disassembled parts in a crate weigh the same and produce no torque. Compression, timing and combustion belong to the arranged whole.

It also survives part replacement. Swap the spark plugs or a piston and you have the same engine, so the engine's identity follows the organization more than any particular part.

Q-3. Half right, and "just" does the damage. Collective agreement holds money up, but the agreement is backed by institutions: taxes payable only in it, courts that enforce debts in it, ledgers, central banks, and network effects that no individual can opt out of. Searle's formula fits: X counts as Y in context C. Most money today is ledger entries anyway, with very little paper.

Institutional facts are real in the way rent and debt are real: they constrain you whether or not you personally believe in them. Hyperinflation shows they depend on conditions being maintained. Fragility is a property of real things, so it gives no reason to call money unreal.

Q-4. A thing, specifically a process-thing. A phantom jam keeps its identity while every car in it changes. Cars enter at the back and leave at the front, and the jam itself often travels backward, against the flow. It has a speed, a size and a lifespan, and traffic-wave models (Lighthill-Whitham-Richards) treat it as a wave. That is the glider again: a persistent pattern moving through a substrate whose members keep changing.

Q-5. Made of words plus structure. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same words and are different sentences with different truth conditions. Syntax is one of the constituents, so the word list underdetermines the sentence.

The levels stack. Words are made of morphemes, and morphemes of phonemes, with structure doing work at every step.

Q-6. Disagree. The argument moves from "can't detect the substrate" to "less real," and that step fails. The character's inability to find the GPU shows that the game level is causally closed: its regularities run on their own terms. We can't find our neurons by introspection either, and that makes our thoughts no less real.

What holds is asymmetric dependence: the game needs some hardware, and the hardware doesn't need the game. Dependence is a different claim from degree of reality. The game can also run on different hardware (multiple realizability), which makes it more independent of any particular substrate than the substrate is of it. By the argument's logic, chemistry would be less real than physics.

Q-7. Yes, under the identity condition rivers actually have. A river is a persistent flow pattern through a basin and channel. The water changing is what being a river consists in. If the water stopped changing, you'd have a pond.

Identity here comes in degrees and has vague edges. Rivers meander, migrate, split, and get dammed into reservoirs, and at some point "same river" stops applying. Year to year, normally, it clearly applies.

Q-8. Unsound. The search treats the self as one more item on the inventory, alongside body parts, thoughts and sensations. If the self is the organization that integrates those over time, an inventory of items will never turn it up, just as inspecting cells never turns up a glider. The search validly shows that the self is no separate component. "No self" needs a further premise, that only components exist, and that premise is the thing in dispute.

To be fair to the Buddhist and Humean versions, their target is usually a permanent, independent self-substance, and against that the argument works well. The sound conclusion: no unchanging self-thing sits among the parts. The self as an ongoing pattern survives.

Q-9. Barely. A pile is close to the zero case of "more than the parts." Rearrange the bricks and it's still a pile with nearly the same properties. What it adds is thin: a total mass, a rough shape, an angle of repose.

That makes it a useful control. Stack the same bricks into an arch and the arrangement carries load the bricks can't carry loose. How much "more" a whole is depends on how much work its organization does, and in a pile it does almost none.

Q-10. The question splits because "ship" carries two identity conditions: continuity of the functioning whole, and sameness of material. Gradual replacement keeps the first, which is why museums and navies count a restored vessel as the original, and why you stay the same organism while your cells turn over. Hobbes's twist, reassembling the discarded planks, satisfies the second.

My call: the gradually repaired ship is Theseus's ship. The reassembled one is a reconstruction from its original material. The old planks lost their continuity as a ship when they were taken out one by one, and continuity of the working pattern is what the concept is mostly for.

Q-11. Wrong. Reduction tells you what temperature is, and a successful identification confirms that it is real. "Just" turns an explanation into an elimination. Compare caloric: it was eliminated because nothing matched it. Temperature was reduced because something did.

Temperature is also an ensemble property. A single molecule has no temperature, so the reduction itself shows a level-specific property. Strictly, temperature is defined through how entropy changes with energy (1/T = ∂S/∂E), which is why spin systems can have negative temperatures. "Molecules moving" is the common realization of temperature. The deeper definition is statistical.

Q-12. Slightly more, and it makes a good contrast with Q-1. A block's stability is relational. Each live cell has exactly three live neighbors, so it survives, and no surrounding dead cell has exactly three, so nothing is born. Scatter the same four cells apart and they all die in one generation. Being a still life belongs to the arrangement.

Unlike the glider, the block keeps the same cells for its whole life, so pattern and material coincide over time. The block is the case where the "more" is pure structure. The glider is the case where the pattern also separates from its material. The block still has pattern-level behavior: in some glider collisions it acts as an eater, and in others it gets destroyed.
