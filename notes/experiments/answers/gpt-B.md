Q-1. Is a glider in Conway’s Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

By its elements: At any instant, a glider consists of five live cells arranged in a particular configuration on the Game of Life grid. Each cell is simply alive or dead.

As a pattern: A glider is a configuration that reproduces its shape, shifted one cell diagonally, every four generations. The individual live cells change, but the moving pattern persists.

The descriptions pick out different things. The same five-cell snapshot can be treated as a static arrangement, whereas the glider is a sequence of arrangements connected by the game’s rules. Conversely, different sets of grid positions can instantiate the same glider pattern.

The question concerns persistence, not merely a snapshot. A glider is real as a dynamically sustained pattern, although it is not an additional physical object beyond the grid and its evolution. Arguing that it is unreal because no individual cell travels diagonally confuses the elements with the pattern. That exposes a weak argument, but the glider’s persistence must still be established from the rules of the game.

Q-2. Is a car engine made of its parts?

By its elements: At an instant, a car engine consists of components such as pistons, cylinders, valves, crankshaft, and associated materials. These are its constituent parts.

As a pattern: An engine is an organized mechanism whose components interact to convert energy into mechanical work. Its functional organization can remain stable even when individual parts are replaced.

The descriptions differ. A pile of engine parts contains many of the same elements but is not an assembled, working engine. Conversely, two engines with different components can instantiate the same mechanical design.

The question primarily concerns composition at a snapshot, although engine identity can persist through repairs. Yes, an engine is made of its parts, but not merely their unordered collection. Their organization and causal interactions matter. Nothing in this answer requires treating the organization as a separate substance.

Q-3. Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a banknote consists of paper or polymer, ink, security features, and other physical materials. But money also includes coins, bank deposits, and digital account balances.

As a pattern: Money is an institutional system in which certain objects and records function as means of payment, units of account, and stores of value. These functions can persist while the physical tokens and records change.

The descriptions pick out different things. A counterfeit note may have nearly identical physical elements without being legitimate money. Conversely, a bank deposit can function as money without being paper at all.

The question concerns both a snapshot of a monetary object and the persistence of a social institution. Money is socially constituted, but it is not merely paper or arbitrary private belief. It depends on enforceable rules, shared practices, institutions, and economic relationships. Its dependence on collective recognition does not make it unreal.

Q-4. Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

By its elements: At an instant, a traffic jam consists of vehicles occupying road space, moving slowly or remaining stationary, together with the drivers and conditions affecting them.

As a pattern: A traffic jam is a persistent configuration of high vehicle density and restricted traffic flow. A congestion wave can move backward along a road even as individual vehicles move forward through it.

The descriptions pick out different things. The same cars can be present on a road without producing congestion. Conversely, a traffic jam can persist while all the cars originally involved eventually leave and are replaced by others.

The question concerns persistence through changing constituents. A traffic jam is real as a collective dynamical phenomenon, not as an additional vehicle-like object. Rejecting its reality merely because it cannot be found among the individual cars confuses a pattern with its constituents. That argument is weak, although whether a particular jam persists must be determined by continuity of traffic conditions.

Q-5. Is a sentence made of words?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a written sentence consists of visible marks arranged into words, punctuation, and spaces. A spoken sentence consists of temporally organized speech sounds.

As a pattern: A sentence is a structured linguistic expression with syntactic relationships and potentially meaningful content. The same sentence can be realized in different handwriting, fonts, voices, or physical media.

The descriptions differ. The words “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” contain the same words but express different relationships. Conversely, the same sentence can be spoken by different people using entirely different sound waves.

The question primarily concerns composition and organization, although a sentence type can persist across different instances. Yes, a sentence is made of words in the relevant linguistic sense, but its identity depends on their order and grammatical relationships. It cannot be adequately characterized as a mere unordered collection.

Q-6. A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

By its elements: At a physical snapshot, the game runs through hardware states involving memory, processors, and other electronic components. The game world’s represented objects are encoded in these states.

As a pattern: The game world consists of structured computational states and rules governing objects, locations, events, and interactions. Its organization can persist across hardware changes or be instantiated on different machines.

The descriptions pick out different things. Two computers with different components can run equivalent instances of the same game world. Conversely, the same computer can run entirely different game worlds without changing its hardware.

The question concerns persistence and levels of description, not simply physical composition. I disagree with the inference. A character’s inability to encounter the graphics card is unsurprising because the card belongs to the implementation level, not the represented world. Claiming that the game world is unreal because it is absent among the hardware components confuses these levels. This exposes a weak argument, but does not independently prove that a particular game world persists or that its objects possess the same kind of reality as physical objects.

Q-7. Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a river consists of flowing water, suspended materials, and the physical channel and surrounding conditions that shape its course.

As a pattern: A river is an ongoing hydrological system sustained by sources, drainage networks, gravity, and continuous water flow. Its organization can remain recognizable even though its water molecules are continually replaced.

The descriptions differ. The same water molecules might be collected in a reservoir without constituting the original river. Conversely, a river can retain its identity despite containing almost none of the water it held a year earlier.

The question concerns persistence. Usually, yes: it is the same river through continuity of its hydrological and geographical organization. However, major changes in course, fragmentation, or drainage can create ambiguous cases. Its identity is not established simply by finding similar water, but by tracing the continuity of the river system.

Q-8. Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a person exhibits bodily processes, sensations, thoughts, emotions, memories, and neural activity. None of these individual elements necessarily appears as a distinct, independently existing self.

As a pattern: A self can be understood as an organized and temporally continuous process involving memory, bodily regulation, perspective, agency, and relationships with the world. Its component processes change while aspects of personal organization persist.

The descriptions can pick out different things. A single thought is not a whole person, and the disappearance of that thought need not eliminate the person’s self. Conversely, a continuing person can undergo substantial changes in memories, beliefs, and bodily composition.

The question concerns persistence and whether a self exists independently of its constituents. The argument is not sound without additional premises. Failing to find a separate self among experiences does not establish that no organized self exists. It assumes that anything real must appear as one more element in the inventory. This exposes a weak argument, but does not demonstrate that a persistent self exists. Establishing persistence requires independent criteria, and whether there is a substantial, unchanging self remains a separate philosophical question.

Q-9. Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a pile consists of individual bricks occupying positions in space, together with the physical relationships between them.

As a pattern: A pile is an arrangement of objects grouped by proximity, contact, and accumulation. Its identity can sometimes be maintained as bricks are added, removed, or rearranged.

The descriptions differ modestly. The same bricks distributed among several buildings would not constitute the original pile. Conversely, a pile may remain recognizable after some original bricks have been replaced.

The question mainly concerns snapshot composition, though it can also involve persistence. A pile is more than an unordered inventory of bricks in the sense that arrangement matters, but it need not be an additional entity with independent causal powers. Its boundaries and identity conditions are comparatively loose. Unlike a tightly coordinated mechanism, a pile may exhibit little functional organization beyond physical accumulation.

Q-10. If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

By its elements: At any instant, the ship consists of planks, fasteners, structural components, and other materials assembled into a seaworthy vessel.

As a pattern: The ship is a continuing organized structure with a particular history, design, function, and sequence of repairs. Its form and operational continuity can persist while its materials are gradually replaced.

The descriptions differ. The original planks can be removed and reconstructed into another vessel, preserving the materials but interrupting the original ship’s continuous history. Conversely, a maintained ship can preserve its organizational and historical continuity after every original plank has been replaced.

The question concerns persistence rather than snapshot composition. Under a continuity-based account, yes, it is the same ship. Under a strict material-identity account, it is not. If the discarded planks are reassembled into another ship, the competing criteria produce the classic Ship of Theseus puzzle. Neither a snapshot inventory nor the mere assertion of a persistent pattern settles which identity criterion is appropriate.

Q-11. Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

By its elements: At a microscopic snapshot, a material system contains particles with particular positions, velocities, and interactions. In appropriate classical systems, temperature is related to the statistical distribution of particle energies, not simply to the movement of a single molecule.

As a pattern: Temperature is a macroscopic thermodynamic property that characterizes systems in thermal equilibrium and helps predict heat transfer and other physical behavior. It remains meaningful across changes in individual molecular positions and velocities.

The descriptions differ. Two samples can have different microscopic configurations yet the same temperature. Conversely, systems with similar total kinetic energies may have different temperatures because of differences in particle numbers, interactions, or available degrees of freedom.

The question concerns whether a macroscopic property is real, rather than the persistence of a particular object. No, temperature is real. It is a measurable physical property grounded in microscopic behavior. Arguing that temperature is unreal because no individual molecule is itself a temperature confuses microscopic constituents with a statistical property of the system. This exposes a weak argument, although the existence and applicability of temperature must still be justified by thermodynamic theory and empirical evidence, not by the pattern distinction alone.

Q-12. Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

By its elements: At a snapshot, a block consists of four live cells arranged in a 2×2 square, surrounded by cells whose states allow that arrangement to remain stable.

As a pattern: A block is a stationary configuration that reproduces itself unchanged under successive applications of the Game of Life rules. Unlike a glider, it does not move or cycle through different shapes.

The descriptions are closely related but conceptually distinct. Four isolated live cells arranged differently do not form a block. Conversely, the same block pattern can occur at different grid locations, using different cells.

The question concerns both a snapshot and persistence across generations. A block is more than an unordered collection of four live cells, because its spatial organization and stability under the rules matter. But it is not an additional object over and above the configured cells and their evolution. Since the cells of a block do not change state, the elements and pattern coincide particularly closely over time. That does not make the pattern unreal; nor does identifying the pattern alone establish its persistence. Its stability follows from the Game of Life rules.
