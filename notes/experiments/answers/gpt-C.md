Q-1. Is a glider in Conway’s Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

Assume that real means something with an identifiable structure, persistence, and causal significance, rather than necessarily something made of unchanging material. Under that assumption, a glider is real as a pattern. It moves across the grid even though individual cells repeatedly turn on and off.

The alternative is to say that only the cells and their changing states exist, while the glider is merely our description. That view captures the underlying mechanism but overlooks the stable organization that makes a glider recognizable and allows us to predict its interactions.

The deciding issue is what counts as existence. If only fundamental components count, the glider is not an additional substance. If organized patterns count, it is real. The glider is a real pattern, not a separate material object.

Q-2. Is a car engine made of its parts?

Assume that made of refers to physical composition. Yes, an engine consists of components such as pistons, cylinders, valves, and a crankshaft. But merely collecting those parts does not produce a functioning engine. Their arrangement and interactions matter.

One view holds that the engine is nothing beyond its parts and their physical relationships. Another emphasizes that the assembled system has properties, such as producing mechanical power, that individual components lack.

These views need not conflict. The engine’s functions arise from the organized components without requiring an additional mysterious substance. An engine is made of its parts, but its identity and operation depend on their organization.

Q-3. Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

Assume we’re discussing paper currency rather than all forms of money. A banknote is physically paper or another material, but its monetary status depends on institutions, laws, established practices, and collective acceptance. Its value is not an intrinsic property of the material.

One alternative says that money is merely a shared fiction. Another holds that institutional facts are genuinely real because they produce observable consequences: money can settle debts, purchase goods, and influence behavior. Moreover, legal obligations and monetary systems can persist even when particular individuals stop believing in them.

The important distinction is between physical existence and social existence. Money is socially constituted, but that does not make it imaginary. And money is not always paper: bank deposits and other monetary forms illustrate the point.

Q-4. Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

Assume that a thing need not be a separately bounded physical object. A traffic jam is a collective condition involving vehicles, their spacing, speeds, interactions, and the road environment. It can persist even while individual vehicles enter and leave.

The reductionist alternative says that there is nothing beyond the cars and their movements. That is plausible at the level of physical composition, but the jam also has system-level features. For example, congestion waves can propagate backward through traffic even while cars move forward.

The deciding consideration is whether a phenomenon can have stable, measurable properties without being an independent substance. Traffic jams clearly can. A traffic jam is a real collective phenomenon, constituted by interacting vehicles rather than existing separately from them.

Q-5. Is a sentence made of words?

Assume we’re discussing an ordinary linguistic sentence. Words are among its constituents, but a sentence also involves grammatical structure, ordering, and relationships among expressions. The same words arranged differently can communicate different meanings.

An alternative view treats a sentence as nothing more than a sequence of words. For some purposes, such as counting words, this is adequate. For interpreting meaning, however, syntax and context are indispensable. Even a one-word utterance can sometimes function as a complete sentence.

Thus, the answer depends on the level of description. A sentence is composed of linguistic elements, but its structure and meaning are not captured by simply listing its words. No additional physical substance is required for those linguistic properties to be real.

Q-6. A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

Assume we’re distinguishing physical reality from simulated reality. The graphics card is a physical object, while the game world is a computationally implemented environment. A character cannot ordinarily discover the graphics card by exploring the simulated landscape because the card is not represented as an object within that landscape.

One interpretation concludes that the game world is therefore less real. Another argues that this confuses different levels of description. The game world is real as a simulation, even though its mountains, buildings, and inhabitants are not physical objects of the kinds they represent.

The character’s inability to find the hardware does not establish the game world’s degree of reality; it reflects the distinction between the simulated environment and its implementation. The hardware is physically real, and the game world is real as an implemented simulation. Whether the latter is less real depends on what standard of reality we adopt.

Q-7. Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

Assume that identity over time need not require retaining exactly the same material. A river continually exchanges water, transports sediment, changes its banks, and varies in flow. Nevertheless, we ordinarily regard it as the same river because of continuity in its course, watershed, and hydrological processes.

An alternative demands strict material identity: if the water has changed, the river cannot be exactly the same collection of matter. This is internally coherent but poorly matches how we identify rivers.

What would decide between these views is the intended criterion of identity. If we mean the same water molecules, generally not. If we mean the continuing geographical and hydrological system, generally yes. A river persists through organized continuity, not through the permanent retention of its material components.

Q-8. Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

Assume the argument means that careful introspection reveals bodily sensations, perceptions, thoughts, and feelings, but no separate, independently existing observer behind them. This is a serious philosophical observation, associated with Buddhist no-self traditions and some Western philosophical approaches.

However, the conclusion does not follow automatically. Failure to find a distinct self through introspection establishes, at most, that no such self was detected using that method. It does not prove that every possible conception of self is false. A self might instead be an organized process involving memory, embodiment, agency, and psychological continuity.

The alternative is that the absence of a separately observable self is precisely what we should expect if the self is a process rather than an independent object. Distinguishing these interpretations requires specifying what a self is supposed to be and what evidence would establish its existence.

The argument challenges the idea of a separate, substantial self, but it does not by itself establish that no self exists in any sense. Its conclusion is stronger than its premise supports.

Q-9. Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

Assume that more refers to something beyond the constituent material. A pile consists of bricks arranged in a particular spatial relationship. The bricks could also be stacked into a wall or spread individually across a field. Their material would remain, but the pile would disappear.

A strict reductionist might say that the pile is nothing but the bricks and their positions. An alternative treats the pile as a genuine composite object with properties such as overall shape, height, stability, and weight distribution.

Both interpretations can accommodate the physical evidence. Their disagreement concerns whether an organized collection deserves recognition as an object in its own right. A pile is not an additional substance beyond its bricks, but its arrangement makes it a distinguishable whole. Calling it real does not require denying that it is constituted by its parts.

Q-10. If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

Assume that the replacements occur gradually while the ship maintains its structure, function, and continuous history. Under an ordinary practical criterion, we would likely regard it as the same ship despite the complete replacement of its original planks.

An alternative adopts material continuity as decisive. On that view, the replacement ship cannot be numerically identical to the original because none of its original material remains. The tension becomes clearer if someone collects all the discarded planks and reconstructs another ship. Which one is the original?

There is no universally accepted answer because competing theories of identity use different criteria. Continuous history favors the maintained ship; original material favors the reconstructed one. The ship can remain the same under a continuity-based criterion, but not under a criterion requiring retention of its original material. The paradox reveals the need to clarify what makes an object persist.

Q-11. Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

Assume we mean the temperature of ordinary matter understood through statistical mechanics. Temperature is related to the distribution of energy among microscopic degrees of freedom. For an ideal gas, average molecular translational kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature, though the relationship is more complicated in other systems.

One interpretation claims that because temperature is explained microscopically, it has no independent reality. The alternative distinguishes between being fundamentally independent and being real. Temperature has measurable consequences: it governs heat transfer, affects material behavior, and helps determine equilibrium.

Microscopic explanation does not eliminate these macroscopic properties. Indeed, statistical mechanics helps explain why temperature is a reliable physical quantity. Temperature is real as a thermodynamic property, even though it depends on microscopic physical processes. Being explainable in terms of something else does not make a phenomenon unreal.

Q-12. Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

Assume we mean the standard block of live cells in Conway’s Game of Life. Under the game’s rules, each of its four live cells has three live neighbors, so all survive. Adjacent dead cells do not have exactly three live neighbors, so none become alive. The pattern remains unchanged.

One view says the block is simply four live cells. Another says that what makes it a block is not merely the number of cells but their arrangement and resulting stability. Four cells arranged differently need not form a still life.

Unlike a glider, a block does not maintain its identity by moving through successive cell locations. Its stability is tied to a fixed spatial configuration. Nevertheless, both are identifiable patterns governed by the same rules.

The block is not a fifth entity added to four cells, but it is more than an unstructured collection of four live cells. Its organization explains its persistence, making it a real, stable pattern within the Game of Life.
