P-1. Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

A traffic jam is a real phenomenon, but it is not a separate physical object alongside the cars. It is a collective pattern arising from the positions, speeds, and interactions of vehicles on a road.

The same traffic jam can persist even as individual cars enter and leave it. This suggests that its identity depends on the continuity of a pattern rather than on the identity of its constituent vehicles.

So a traffic jam is not just cars in the sense of an arbitrary collection. It is an organized, dynamically maintained condition of traffic. Its reality does not require it to be an independent substance.

P-2. If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

There is no universally agreed answer. If the planks are replaced gradually while the ship continues operating, we have good reasons to regard it as the same ship. Its structure, function, history, and continuity have been preserved.

However, if numerical identity requires retaining the same original material, then it is no longer the same ship. The difficulty becomes especially clear if the discarded planks are reassembled into another ship: which one is the original?

The Ship of Theseus illustrates that identity over time depends partly on the criteria we adopt. Material continuity, structural continuity, and historical continuity can yield different answers. That does not make identity arbitrary, but it does show that the concept has more than one plausible application.

P-3. Is a glider in Conway’s Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

A glider is a real pattern within the mathematical system called Conway’s Game of Life. It consists of a recurring configuration of live and dead cells that moves across the grid through successive generations.

Strictly speaking, no individual cell moves. Cells change states according to fixed rules, and the overall configuration reappears at displaced positions. Nevertheless, describing the glider as moving is accurate at the level of patterns.

The glider’s reality is therefore not that of an additional physical object. It is a higher-level structure defined by relationships and evolution over time. Its existence is compatible with the fact that the underlying system consists entirely of cells and rules.

P-4. Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

Ordinarily, yes. A river remains the same river even though its water is continuously replaced. Its identity is maintained through continuity of flow, geography, drainage system, and history rather than through the persistence of particular water molecules.

This makes a river more like an ongoing process than a static object. Its banks can shift, its course can change, and its volume can fluctuate while it remains recognizably the same river.

There are limits, however. If a river permanently changes course or divides into separate channels, questions about its identity become harder. The river is real, but its persistence depends on the continuity of an organized process rather than an unchanging collection of material.

U-1. Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

Partly, but the description is incomplete. Money is not simply paper: it is a social and economic institution in which certain objects, records, or balances function as means of payment, stores of value, and units of account.

A banknote has physical properties, but its monetary value depends on social practices, institutional arrangements, legal systems, and collective expectations. Digital money makes this particularly clear because money need not have a physical token at all.

Yet socially constituted does not mean imaginary. Money has real causal effects: it enables transactions, creates obligations, influences behavior, and shapes economic activity. Its reality is institutional rather than purely physical, and the agreements supporting it can be implicit, legally enforced, or maintained through established practices.

U-2. Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

Not as a deductive argument. Failing to find a separate self among bodily sensations, thoughts, and perceptions does not establish that no self exists. It may establish only that the self is not the kind of independently identifiable object being sought.

The reasoning risks assuming that anything real must appear as a distinct item within experience. But the self might instead be an organized process, a continuity of memory and agency, or an integrated system of bodily and psychological capacities.

The observation is nevertheless philosophically significant. It challenges the idea of a permanent, unchanging inner observer separate from experience. A more defensible conclusion is that introspection does not reveal such an observer—not that every possible conception of self has been disproved.

U-3. A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

Not on that reasoning alone. A character’s inability to find the graphics card is unsurprising because the graphics card belongs to a different level of description from the simulated world.

The game world’s objects and events are implemented by physical hardware and software, but they can still have definite properties and relationships within the simulation. A virtual mountain can be a genuine feature of the game even though it is not a physical mountain.

The hardware and the game world have different modes of existence. Hardware exists physically; the game world exists as a computationally implemented structure. Calling the latter less real may express its dependence on the former, but dependence does not automatically imply unreality. Nor does the character’s limited access establish an ontological hierarchy.

U-4. Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

No. Explaining temperature in terms of microscopic physical processes does not eliminate temperature. It explains how this macroscopic property arises.

Temperature is related to the statistical distribution of energy in a system. In familiar gases, molecular motion is central to that explanation, although temperature is not simply identical to the speed of individual molecules.

Temperature is measurable, supports reliable predictions, and helps explain heat transfer and thermodynamic behavior. Its dependence on microscopic processes does not make it illusory. Rather, it illustrates how a higher-level property can be real without being an additional substance beyond its physical basis.

N-1. Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

A pile of bricks consists of bricks, but its properties also depend on how they are arranged. The same bricks could form a wall, an arch, or a scattered collection, with very different structural and functional consequences.

The pile need not introduce a new substance beyond the bricks. Nevertheless, its shape, stability, height, and spatial relationships are properties of the collection that cannot be attributed to an individual brick in isolation.

Whether the pile counts as an additional object is a separate metaphysical question. One can recognize the reality of the arrangement without committing to the existence of a fundamentally new entity over and above the arranged bricks.

N-2. Is a car engine made of its parts?

Yes. A car engine is composed of physical parts such as pistons, cylinders, valves, and a crankshaft. But merely assembling a collection of these parts in one location does not necessarily produce a functioning engine.

The parts must be organized and connected in appropriate ways. Their interactions enable processes such as compression, combustion, and mechanical energy transfer. Engine operation therefore depends on both the components and their organization.

This does not require a mysterious additional ingredient beyond the physical system. The engine is a structured whole whose capacities arise from the coordinated operation of its parts. Composition explains what it is made of; organization helps explain what it can do.

N-3. Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

A block consists of four adjacent live cells arranged in a 2×2 square. Under Conway’s Game of Life rules, each cell remains alive and the surrounding dead cells remain dead, so the configuration persists unchanged.

The block has no independent substance beyond the cells and their states. However, its stability is a property of the entire configuration under the update rules, not merely of four isolated live cells. Four differently arranged live cells need not form a still life.

Thus, the block is more than an arbitrary collection in an organizational sense, but not necessarily in a fundamental ontological sense. It is a genuine stable pattern constituted by the arrangement of cells and the rules governing them.

N-4. Is a sentence made of words?

Yes, although a sentence is not merely an unordered collection of words. Word order, grammatical structure, and syntactic relationships are essential to its identity and meaning.

For example, “The dog chased the cat” and “The cat chased the dog” contain exactly the same words but communicate different events. The difference arises from how those words are organized.

A sentence also depends on linguistic conventions that assign meanings and grammatical roles to its elements. It is therefore a structured linguistic entity composed of words, with properties that depend on their arrangement and interpretation. Recognizing those properties does not require treating the sentence as a separate substance.

Overall philosophical significance

These twelve cases illuminate three closely related but distinct questions:

* P — Persistence: How can something remain the same while its constituents change? Traffic jams, ships, gliders, and rivers illustrate identity through continuity of organization, function, or process.
* U — Underlying reality: Does explaining something through a more fundamental basis make it unreal? Money, the self, virtual worlds, and temperature show why dependence, reduction, and nonexistence must be distinguished.
* N — Nature of composition: Is a whole nothing more than its parts? Bricks, engines, still lifes, and sentences show that composition involves relationships and organization as well as constituents.

A common principle emerges: being dependent on something else for existence does not, by itself, make a phenomenon unreal, and being composed of parts does not make organization irrelevant.

However, these examples do not establish that every higher-level pattern is an independent entity. They support a more careful distinction between what exists, what constitutes it, how it persists, and which level of description best explains its behavior.
