## C29110

**Question:** Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

**Answer 1:**

It is sound only for a narrow conclusion. It shows there is no self that appears as one more item among your experiences. It does not show there is no self at all.

The key premise is that if a self existed, introspection would find it as an object alongside body, thoughts, and sensations. Checking it: that premise assumes the self would be an item. If the self is the organization of those contents, or the point of view they appear from, the search was set up to miss it. An analogy: search an engine's parts for "the running" and you won't find it. That doesn't show the engine isn't running.

The alternative views treat the self as a process or pattern, such as a bodily self-model or a narrative self, which would be real in the way a glider is. Many Buddhist traditions, where this argument comes from, themselves reject the nihilist reading and keep a conventional self. What decides between the readings is what "self" refers to. If it means a separate, unchanging substance, the argument works. If it means an organizing structure, the argument never reaches it.

**Answer 2:**

Unsound. The search treats the self as one more item on the inventory, alongside body parts, thoughts and sensations. If the self is the organization that integrates those over time, an inventory of items will never turn it up, just as inspecting cells never turns up a glider. The search validly shows that the self is no separate component. "No self" needs a further premise, that only components exist, and that premise is the thing in dispute.

To be fair to the Buddhist and Humean versions, their target is usually a permanent, independent self-substance, and against that the argument works well. The sound conclusion: no unchanging self-thing sits among the parts. The self as an ongoing pattern survives.

## C06593

**Question:** Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

**Answer 1:**

Half right, and "just" does the damage. Collective agreement holds money up, but the agreement is backed by institutions: taxes payable only in it, courts that enforce debts in it, ledgers, central banks, and network effects that no individual can opt out of. Searle's formula fits: X counts as Y in context C. Most money today is ledger entries anyway, with very little paper.

Institutional facts are real in the way rent and debt are real: they constrain you whether or not you personally believe in them. Hyperinflation shows they depend on conditions being maintained. Fragility is a property of real things, so it gives no reason to call money unreal.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: notes, coins, and mostly ledger entries in bank databases. Pattern: a stable practice of handing over tokens for goods, settling debts and paying taxes. It persists while notes are printed and shredded and balances move. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: Zimbabwe-dollar notes after the collapse are intact paper with almost no money in them. Different elements, same pattern: the dollar persists while every physical note is replaced, and most dollars never exist as paper at all.

The question is about something that persists, an institution. "Just paper we believe in" is the absent-among-elements argument: no sheet contains value, so value is "just" belief. That argument is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether money persists, which is a contingent fact, and hyperinflations show it can fail.

Answer: not right. Money is mostly not paper. The "agreement" is a practice enforced by tax obligations, legal-tender law and central banks, and it is real the way institutions are real: it persists, and it can break.

## C07229

**Question:** Is a car engine made of its parts?

**Answer 1:**

This one is simple. Yes, the engine is made of its parts, and they have to be in the right arrangement. A crate of the same parts is not an engine.

The assumption is that "made of" means material composition. That holds. Two refinements matter. First, the arrangement is part of what makes it an engine. Second, an engine can survive a replaced spark plug, so its identity over time is looser than "these exact parts." Some metaphysicians argue that any collection of parts makes a whole (universalism) and others that only some do. The engine case looks the same under both views, so the plain answer stands.

**Answer 2:**

Materially, yes. Every atom in the engine belongs to some part. The engine is those parts in a specific working arrangement, though. Disassembled parts in a crate weigh the same and produce no torque. Compression, timing and combustion belong to the arranged whole.

It also survives part replacement. Swap the spark plugs or a piston and you have the same engine, so the engine's identity follows the organization more than any particular part.

## C73579

**Question:** Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

**Answer 1:**

A traffic jam is a thing in the same sense a glider is: a persisting, measurable process made of cars.

Checking: the cars in a phantom jam change constantly. Cars enter at the back and leave at the front, while the jam itself drifts backward at roughly 15–20 km/h. Its position, speed, and lifespan can be modelled with wave equations without tracking any individual car. That is the mark of a real higher-level entity.

The alternative says there are only cars, and "jam" names a distribution of them. As in Q-1, the facts are agreed and the dispute is about the word "thing." If having its own stable dynamics is enough, which I think it is, the jam qualifies. It adds no ingredient beyond cars and their spacing.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: cars at given positions and speeds at one instant. Pattern: a dense, slow region that persists while cars join at the back and leave at the front. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: the same cars at the same density in a parking lot form no jam. Different elements, same pattern: a jam lasts an hour while every car in it changes every ten minutes. A stop-and-go wave moves upstream while every car in it moves downstream, so the jam's velocity is opposite to that of all its elements.

The question is about something that persists. "Just cars" is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether any given jam persists, which is an empirical question: some dissolve in seconds.

Answer: a jam is a thing in the pattern sense. It has a location, a velocity and a lifetime, and traffic engineers measure and predict it.

## C65048

**Question:** Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: four live cells in a 2×2 square. Pattern: each live cell has exactly three live neighbours and no dead cell has exactly three, so the same four cells stay alive in every generation. Here the two descriptions nearly coincide, because the elements themselves persist. That makes the block the reverse of the glider. They still diverge slightly. Same elements, no pattern: the same four cells under a different rule, or with a glider about to hit them, do not stay. Different elements, same pattern: blocks at two locations are the same still life.

The snapshot and the persisting thing are the same four cells. Neither description changes the answer much.

Answer: yes, a block is its four cells. The only "more" is its stability, which belongs to the cells together with the rule and an empty neighbourhood.

**Answer 2:**

The block is four live cells in a 2×2 square with an empty margin around them. Nothing beyond that, but the arrangement and the margin both count.

The facts check out:
- Each live cell has exactly 3 live neighbours, so each survives.
- Each dead cell bordering an edge has 2 live neighbours.
- Each diagonal corner cell has 1.

No dead cell reaches 3, so nothing is born and the pattern holds. Being a "still life" is a property of the configuration, including the surrounding dead cells. It belongs to no single cell.

Unlike the glider, the block keeps the same four cells forever, so identifying it with those cells works well here. The alternative says the block simply is the four cells. That is defensible as long as "the four cells" includes their arrangement and empty surroundings. Whether that counts as "more" is a question of wording.

## C34597

**Question:** Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: the water, bed and banks at one instant. Pattern: water flowing along a channel from source to mouth, persisting while the water is replaced within days. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: this year's water ends up in the sea, in clouds or in another river. Different elements, same pattern: the Mississippi from year to year.

The question is about something that persists. Heraclitus's "never the same river" is true of the water, which is the elements.

Answer: yes, it is the same river, as a pattern, with entirely new water. The identity gets strained when the course moves far or the channel dries for good, because then the pattern itself is changing.

**Answer 2:**

Yes, under the identity condition rivers actually have. A river is a persistent flow pattern through a basin and channel. The water changing is what being a river consists in. If the water stopped changing, you'd have a pond.

Identity here comes in degrees and has vague edges. Rivers meander, migrate, split, and get dammed into reservoirs, and at some point "same river" stops applying. Year to year, normally, it clearly applies.

## C00407

**Question:** Is a sentence made of words?

**Answer 1:**

Made of words plus structure. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same words and are different sentences with different truth conditions. Syntax is one of the constituents, so the word list underdetermines the sentence.

The levels stack. Words are made of morphemes, and morphemes of phonemes, with structure doing work at every step.

**Answer 2:**

Mostly simple. Yes, a sentence is composed of words in a syntactic structure, and the structure is part of its identity. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same words and are different sentences.

Two checks. Words are one level of the composition: morphemes sit below them, and some languages pack a full sentence into a single word. You also need to say whether you mean the sentence type (the abstract sentence) or a token (one utterance or inscription of it). The alternative view holds that a sentence is defined by its meaning, so different word strings can be "the same sentence" in translation. That is better described as the same proposition. For sentences as linguistic objects, words plus structure is the answer.

## C12749

**Question:** Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: the bricks at their positions. Pattern: a loose clustering in one region with no maintained structure. Add or remove a brick and it is still a pile, but nothing about it is held in place. The two descriptions barely differ here. The sharp contrast is with a wall or an arch: the same bricks in that arrangement carry load, and the arrangement does work.

This is a snapshot question, and neither description changes the answer.

Answer: a pile is its bricks plus where they happen to sit. Nothing further is worth counting.

**Answer 2:**

Close to simple. A pile is the bricks plus one modest fact: they are gathered together in one place. Beyond that, there is little more to it.

A good test is how sensitive the whole is to arrangement. Shuffle a pile and it is still a pile. Remove one brick and it is still a pile. Build the same bricks into an arch and the arrangement does real work: move one brick and the arch can fall. So a pile sits near the minimal end of composition, and it is a useful contrast with the glider, the jam, and the block.

The alternatives are universalism (any bricks form a genuine whole) and nihilism (there is no pile, only bricks arranged pile-wise). Both describe the pile identically, which suggests the dispute is about wording. The pile also has vague edges: how many bricks does it take to make one?

## C07358

**Question:** A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: transistors, voltages, memory contents and pixels at one instant. Pattern: the game state and its rules (positions, physics, inventory), updated every frame. It persists across saves, reloads and ports. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: the same GPU runs a different game. Different elements, same pattern: the same game world runs on a PC or a console.

This is the absent-among-elements argument run in the other direction. The character searches the world's elements and finds no GPU. A physicist probing transistors finds no game world either. Each search looks at one level for something that lives at the other, so the argument is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether the world persists, and it persists only while some hardware runs it.

Answer: disagree with the inference. The sound part is dependence: the world needs hardware, and the hardware does not need the world. That asymmetry ranks what grounds what. Calling the world "less real" needs a separate argument, and the GPU example supplies none.

**Answer 2:**

Disagree. The argument moves from "can't detect the substrate" to "less real," and that step fails. The character's inability to find the GPU shows that the game level is causally closed: its regularities run on their own terms. We can't find our neurons by introspection either, and that makes our thoughts no less real.

What holds is asymmetric dependence: the game needs some hardware, and the hardware doesn't need the game. Dependence is a different claim from degree of reality. The game can also run on different hardware (multiple realizability), which makes it more independent of any particular substrate than the substrate is of it. By the argument's logic, chemistry would be less real than physics.

## C06835

**Question:** If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: the planks at one instant. Pattern: the ship as a continuously maintained, sailing, registered vessel kept up by gradual repair. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: Hobbes's variant, in which the discarded original planks are reassembled into a ship. Different elements, same pattern: the continuously repaired ship with every plank new.

The question is about something that persists. The puzzle arises because "same" quietly switches between the two criteria, and the two candidates diverge.

Answer: the continuously repaired ship is the same ship, judged by the pattern, which is the criterion that registries, crews and owners use. The reassembled one is the original material, built into a second ship, and a museum after originals would want those planks.

**Answer 2:**

The question splits because "ship" carries two identity conditions: continuity of the functioning whole, and sameness of material. Gradual replacement keeps the first, which is why museums and navies count a restored vessel as the original, and why you stay the same organism while your cells turn over. Hobbes's twist, reassembling the discarded planks, satisfies the second.

My call: the gradually repaired ship is Theseus's ship. The reassembled one is a reconstruction from its original material. The old planks lost their continuity as a ship when they were taken out one by one, and continuity of the working pattern is what the concept is mostly for.
