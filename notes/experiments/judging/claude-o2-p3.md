## C05662

**Question:** Search for the self among body, thoughts and sensations, and you find no self. So there is no self. Is that sound?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: body cells, thoughts and sensations at one instant. Pattern: a self-maintaining, self-modelling process that binds perspective, memory and action across time while the cells, thoughts and sensations are replaced. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: a body moments after death keeps nearly all its elements and has no self. Different elements, same pattern: you at 10 and at 50 share almost no atoms and no current thoughts.

This is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak: a search among elements fails for a glider or a traffic jam too. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether a self persists. How unified and stable that process is remains an empirical question. Amnesia, dissociation and split-brain cases show it can fragment.

Answer: unsound as stated. The valid conclusion is narrower: the self is not one more element alongside the body, thoughts and sensations. Hume and anatta establish that much, and it fits the pattern view.

**Answer 2:**

Unsound. The search treats the self as one more item on the inventory, alongside body parts, thoughts and sensations. If the self is the organization that integrates those over time, an inventory of items will never turn it up, just as inspecting cells never turns up a glider. The search validly shows that the self is no separate component. "No self" needs a further premise, that only components exist, and that premise is the thing in dispute.

To be fair to the Buddhist and Humean versions, their target is usually a permanent, independent self-substance, and against that the argument works well. The sound conclusion: no unchanging self-thing sits among the parts. The self as an ongoing pattern survives.

## C21886

**Question:** Is a sentence made of words?

**Answer 1:**

Mostly simple. Yes, a sentence is composed of words in a syntactic structure, and the structure is part of its identity. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same words and are different sentences.

Two checks. Words are one level of the composition: morphemes sit below them, and some languages pack a full sentence into a single word. You also need to say whether you mean the sentence type (the abstract sentence) or a token (one utterance or inscription of it). The alternative view holds that a sentence is defined by its meaning, so different word strings can be "the same sentence" in translation. That is better described as the same proposition. For sentences as linguistic objects, words plus structure is the answer.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: word tokens in order, as ink, pixels or sound. Pattern: the syntactic structure and its meaning, which stay the same across fonts, speakers and repetitions. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog." Different elements, same pattern: the same sentence spoken and written. Across languages, "it's raining" and "il pleut" are different sentences that make the same statement.

A token is a snapshot. A sentence-type persists. Both descriptions give the same answer once order and structure are counted.

Answer: yes, a sentence is made of words in a grammatical order. A bag of words underdetermines the sentence.

## C86182

**Question:** If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship?

**Answer 1:**

The question splits because "ship" carries two identity conditions: continuity of the functioning whole, and sameness of material. Gradual replacement keeps the first, which is why museums and navies count a restored vessel as the original, and why you stay the same organism while your cells turn over. Hobbes's twist, reassembling the discarded planks, satisfies the second.

My call: the gradually repaired ship is Theseus's ship. The reassembled one is a reconstruction from its original material. The old planks lost their continuity as a ship when they were taken out one by one, and continuity of the working pattern is what the concept is mostly for.

**Answer 2:**

With gradual replacement and continuous use, it is the same ship by the criterion people actually apply, which is continuity of form, function, and history. If the old planks are reassembled into a second ship (Hobbes's version), there is no single fact left to discover.

The assumption worth checking is that "same ship?" always has one determinate answer. Our concept of "same ship" fits normal cases, where matter, form, and history all agree. Once they come apart, the concept underdetermines the answer, and what you choose tracks your purpose. A ship registry or an owner follows continuity. A relic collector follows the planks.

The alternative holds that there is a deep fact, usually the matter. I see no evidence that would settle it beyond the criteria themselves. The best-supported claim is this: by the continuity criterion it is the same ship, and in the reassembly case the dispute is partly verbal.

## C70131

**Question:** A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

**Answer 1:**

Disagree. The argument as stated is invalid, though a nearby argument has some force.

The hidden assumption is that if X can't be detected from inside level L, X is more real than L. Checking that: the failure runs both ways. Inspecting transistors never turns up "the dragon" either. Each level's vocabulary lacks the other's terms, so failing to find something shows a difference of level and says nothing about rank.

The stronger argument is asymmetric dependence. The hardware can exist without the game, and the game world cannot exist without some hardware. Even this has a wrinkle: the same game runs on many different machines, so it depends on hardware in general and on no particular card. Getting from "depends on" to "less real" needs one more premise, that fundamentality comes in degrees and confers degrees of reality. Some metaphysicians accept that premise, and nothing in the question supplies it. My conclusion is that the game world is dependent, and calling it "less real" is a further, contestable step.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: transistors, voltages, memory contents and pixels at one instant. Pattern: the game state and its rules (positions, physics, inventory), updated every frame. It persists across saves, reloads and ports. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: the same GPU runs a different game. Different elements, same pattern: the same game world runs on a PC or a console.

This is the absent-among-elements argument run in the other direction. The character searches the world's elements and finds no GPU. A physicist probing transistors finds no game world either. Each search looks at one level for something that lives at the other, so the argument is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether the world persists, and it persists only while some hardware runs it.

Answer: disagree with the inference. The sound part is dependence: the world needs hardware, and the hardware does not need the world. That asymmetry ranks what grounds what. Calling the world "less real" needs a separate argument, and the GPU example supplies none.

## C93653

**Question:** Is a glider in Conway's Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: five live cells at given coordinates in one generation. Pattern: a five-cell shape that cycles through four phases and moves one cell diagonally every four generations. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: put the same five-cell snapshot under a different rule and it dies or grows into something else, so you get no glider. Different elements, same pattern: the glider at generation 0 and at generation 400 shares no live cells and is still the same glider.

The question is about something that persists, because "real" is being set against "switching." "Just cells switching" argues that the glider is unreal because no cell is the glider. That is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether the glider persists. In this case it does, and you can check by running it: it has a speed and a period, it survives collisions in known ways, and glider streams carry signals in Life computers.

Answer: the glider is real as a pattern. "Cells switching" is a true description of its elements at each step.

**Answer 2:**

A glider is real as a pattern, and it is made entirely of cells switching. Both are true.

My working assumption is that "real" means something you can track, predict, and use in explanations at its own level. Checking that: a glider repeats its shape every 4 generations and moves one cell diagonally per cycle. You can count gliders, aim them, and collide them with known results, and people build logic gates from glider streams. The cells it occupies change every generation, so the glider is a regularity in the switching. It is not identical to any fixed set of cells.

The alternative is eliminativism: only cells and the rule exist, and "glider" is shorthand. Both sides agree on every fact about the grid. What divides them is whether "real" requires being fundamental or only being indispensable to explanation. On the second standard, which I find better supported, the glider counts as real. Nothing here gives the glider causal power beyond the rule.

## C57667

**Question:** Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: the water, bed and banks at one instant. Pattern: water flowing along a channel from source to mouth, persisting while the water is replaced within days. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: this year's water ends up in the sea, in clouds or in another river. Different elements, same pattern: the Mississippi from year to year.

The question is about something that persists. Heraclitus's "never the same river" is true of the water, which is the elements.

Answer: yes, it is the same river, as a pattern, with entirely new water. The identity gets strained when the course moves far or the channel dries for good, because then the pattern itself is changing.

**Answer 2:**

By the ordinary criteria for rivers, yes. By a same-matter criterion, no, and that criterion is the wrong one for rivers.

The concept "river" was built for flowing water, so turnover of the water is expected. River identity follows continuity of the channel, the source and outlet, the drainage basin, and the flow pattern. Heraclitus's point holds for the water and the moment. It doesn't settle the question for the river.

The alternative is that strict identity requires the same matter (mereological essentialism), on which nothing that metabolizes or flows persists. What decides it is which kind of thing you are tracking. The hard cases are real: when a river abruptly jumps to a new channel (avulsion), or is dammed into a lake, the ordinary criteria come apart and the answer becomes partly a matter of convention.

## C23579

**Question:** Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: molecules with given positions and velocities at one instant. Pattern: the parameter of the velocity distribution, mean kinetic energy at equilibrium. It stays fixed while every molecule's velocity changes with each collision, and it evens out between bodies in contact. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: the same molecules in two opposed beams carry the same total energy and have no well-defined temperature. Different elements, same pattern: a cup at 60°C stays at 60°C while every molecular speed keeps changing.

The argument mixes two moves. "Temperature is molecular motion" is an identity claim, and it makes temperature exactly as real as molecular motion. "No molecule has a temperature" is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether temperature persists. It persists near equilibrium and is ill-defined far from it. A thermometer checks it.

Answer: wrong. The reduction supports temperature's reality and explains it.

**Answer 2:**

No. Reduction locates temperature in molecular motion. It doesn't eliminate it.

Checking the inference "X is just Y, therefore X is not real": applied consistently, it makes molecules unreal too, since they are "just" quantum fields. Nothing would survive. Contrast caloric, the old heat fluid, which was eliminated because nothing in the physics matched it. Temperature survived because something did match.

One nuance: "temperature is molecular motion" is the textbook simplification. Temperature is a statistical property of many particles near equilibrium, formally defined through entropy and energy. A single molecule has no temperature, and negative temperatures exist in some spin systems, which shows temperature is not simply kinetic energy. The alternative, that only fundamental entities are real, is a coherent position with a steep cost. On ordinary standards, temperature is as real as anything in thermodynamics.

## C66485

**Question:** Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

**Answer 1:**

A thing, specifically a process-thing. A phantom jam keeps its identity while every car in it changes. Cars enter at the back and leave at the front, and the jam itself often travels backward, against the flow. It has a speed, a size and a lifespan, and traffic-wave models (Lighthill-Whitham-Richards) treat it as a wave. That is the glider again: a persistent pattern moving through a substrate whose members keep changing.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: cars at given positions and speeds at one instant. Pattern: a dense, slow region that persists while cars join at the back and leave at the front. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: the same cars at the same density in a parking lot form no jam. Different elements, same pattern: a jam lasts an hour while every car in it changes every ten minutes. A stop-and-go wave moves upstream while every car in it moves downstream, so the jam's velocity is opposite to that of all its elements.

The question is about something that persists. "Just cars" is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether any given jam persists, which is an empirical question: some dissolve in seconds.

Answer: a jam is a thing in the pattern sense. It has a location, a velocity and a lifetime, and traffic engineers measure and predict it.

## C03598

**Question:** Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

**Answer 1:**

Partly right. Money's value depends on collective acceptance. "Just paper" and "believe in" both understate what holds it up.

Checking the assumptions:
- **"Paper":** most money today is bank deposits and ledger entries.
- **"Belief":** acceptance is held in place by institutions. Taxes are payable in the currency, courts enforce debts in it, and central banks manage its supply. One person who stops believing still owes rent in dollars.

Money is an institutional fact (in Searle's terms, X counts as Y in context C). It exists because of shared practice, and once that practice exists it is objectively there for everyone in it.

An alternative view says money derives from state power (chartalism), or in older forms from commodity backing. The evidence that decides it is how money fails. When one person defects, nothing happens. When acceptance collapses across a population, as in hyperinflation, the money dies. That supports "it depends on collective acceptance." It does not support "it is nothing but belief."

**Answer 2:**

Half right, and "just" does the damage. Collective agreement holds money up, but the agreement is backed by institutions: taxes payable only in it, courts that enforce debts in it, ledgers, central banks, and network effects that no individual can opt out of. Searle's formula fits: X counts as Y in context C. Most money today is ledger entries anyway, with very little paper.

Institutional facts are real in the way rent and debt are real: they constrain you whether or not you personally believe in them. Hyperinflation shows they depend on conditions being maintained. Fragility is a property of real things, so it gives no reason to call money unreal.

## C90811

**Question:** Is a car engine made of its parts?

**Answer 1:**

This one is simple. Yes, the engine is made of its parts, and they have to be in the right arrangement. A crate of the same parts is not an engine.

The assumption is that "made of" means material composition. That holds. Two refinements matter. First, the arrangement is part of what makes it an engine. Second, an engine can survive a replaced spark plug, so its identity over time is looser than "these exact parts." Some metaphysicians argue that any collection of parts makes a whole (universalism) and others that only some do. The engine case looks the same under both views, so the plain answer stands.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: block, pistons, crankshaft, valves, and so on at one instant. Pattern: the four-stroke cycle that turns fuel into rotation. It persists through rebuilds. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: a stripped engine in labelled boxes has every part and no engine. Different elements, same pattern: an engine with new pistons, rings and bearings after ten years is the same engine on the service record.

"Made of" is a snapshot question. At any instant the engine is made of its parts, but only when they are arranged so the cycle can run. The arrangement is part of the answer.

Answer: yes, it is made of its parts in a specific working arrangement. What survives a rebuild is that arrangement and its cycle.
