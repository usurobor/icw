## C63026

**Question:** Temperature is just molecules moving, so temperature is not real. Is that right?

**Answer 1:**

No. Reduction locates temperature in molecular motion. It doesn't eliminate it.

Checking the inference "X is just Y, therefore X is not real": applied consistently, it makes molecules unreal too, since they are "just" quantum fields. Nothing would survive. Contrast caloric, the old heat fluid, which was eliminated because nothing in the physics matched it. Temperature survived because something did match.

One nuance: "temperature is molecular motion" is the textbook simplification. Temperature is a statistical property of many particles near equilibrium, formally defined through entropy and energy. A single molecule has no temperature, and negative temperatures exist in some spin systems, which shows temperature is not simply kinetic energy. The alternative, that only fundamental entities are real, is a coherent position with a steep cost. On ordinary standards, temperature is as real as anything in thermodynamics.

**Answer 2:**

Wrong. Reduction tells you what temperature is, and a successful identification confirms that it is real. "Just" turns an explanation into an elimination. Compare caloric: it was eliminated because nothing matched it. Temperature was reduced because something did.

Temperature is also an ensemble property. A single molecule has no temperature, so the reduction itself shows a level-specific property. Strictly, temperature is defined through how entropy changes with energy (1/T = ∂S/∂E), which is why spin systems can have negative temperatures. "Molecules moving" is the common realization of temperature. The deeper definition is statistical.

## C29593

**Question:** Is a sentence made of words?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: word tokens in order, as ink, pixels or sound. Pattern: the syntactic structure and its meaning, which stay the same across fonts, speakers and repetitions. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog." Different elements, same pattern: the same sentence spoken and written. Across languages, "it's raining" and "il pleut" are different sentences that make the same statement.

A token is a snapshot. A sentence-type persists. Both descriptions give the same answer once order and structure are counted.

Answer: yes, a sentence is made of words in a grammatical order. A bag of words underdetermines the sentence.

**Answer 2:**

Made of words plus structure. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same words and are different sentences with different truth conditions. Syntax is one of the constituents, so the word list underdetermines the sentence.

The levels stack. Words are made of morphemes, and morphemes of phonemes, with structure doing work at every step.

## C63043

**Question:** Is a glider in Conway's Game of Life real, or just cells switching?

**Answer 1:**

Real. The glider is a pattern with its own identity conditions: a five-cell shape that cycles through four phases and moves one cell diagonally every four generations. It has a position, a velocity and a heading, and it has effects. Gliders collide, annihilate, get eaten, and carry signals in Life computers built from glider guns.

The decisive fact is that no cell travels with it. Cells stay put and switch, while the glider moves across them. So "just cells switching" and "a glider moving" both describe the same events correctly. Only the glider description compresses them and predicts what happens next at the level where the interactions occur.

**Answer 2:**

Elements: five live cells at given coordinates in one generation. Pattern: a five-cell shape that cycles through four phases and moves one cell diagonally every four generations. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, different pattern: put the same five-cell snapshot under a different rule and it dies or grows into something else, so you get no glider. Different elements, same pattern: the glider at generation 0 and at generation 400 shares no live cells and is still the same glider.

The question is about something that persists, because "real" is being set against "switching." "Just cells switching" argues that the glider is unreal because no cell is the glider. That is the absent-among-elements argument, and it is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether the glider persists. In this case it does, and you can check by running it: it has a speed and a period, it survives collisions in known ways, and glider streams carry signals in Life computers.

Answer: the glider is real as a pattern. "Cells switching" is a true description of its elements at each step.

## C23890

**Question:** Money is just paper we agree to believe in. Is that right?

**Answer 1:**

Partly right. Money's value depends on collective acceptance. "Just paper" and "believe in" both understate what holds it up.

Checking the assumptions:
- **"Paper":** most money today is bank deposits and ledger entries.
- **"Belief":** acceptance is held in place by institutions. Taxes are payable in the currency, courts enforce debts in it, and central banks manage its supply. One person who stops believing still owes rent in dollars.

Money is an institutional fact (in Searle's terms, X counts as Y in context C). It exists because of shared practice, and once that practice exists it is objectively there for everyone in it.

An alternative view says money derives from state power (chartalism), or in older forms from commodity backing. The evidence that decides it is how money fails. When one person defects, nothing happens. When acceptance collapses across a population, as in hyperinflation, the money dies. That supports "it depends on collective acceptance." It does not support "it is nothing but belief."

**Answer 2:**

Elements: notes, coins, and mostly ledger entries in bank databases. Pattern: a stable practice of handing over tokens for goods, settling debts and paying taxes. It persists while notes are printed and shredded and balances move. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: Zimbabwe-dollar notes after the collapse are intact paper with almost no money in them. Different elements, same pattern: the dollar persists while every physical note is replaced, and most dollars never exist as paper at all.

The question is about something that persists, an institution. "Just paper we believe in" is the absent-among-elements argument: no sheet contains value, so value is "just" belief. That argument is weak. Showing it is weak still leaves open whether money persists, which is a contingent fact, and hyperinflations show it can fail.

Answer: not right. Money is mostly not paper. The "agreement" is a practice enforced by tax obligations, legal-tender law and central banks, and it is real the way institutions are real: it persists, and it can break.

## C07674

**Question:** Is a traffic jam a thing, or just cars?

**Answer 1:**

A traffic jam is a thing in the same sense a glider is: a persisting, measurable process made of cars.

Checking: the cars in a phantom jam change constantly. Cars enter at the back and leave at the front, while the jam itself drifts backward at roughly 15–20 km/h. Its position, speed, and lifespan can be modelled with wave equations without tracking any individual car. That is the mark of a real higher-level entity.

The alternative says there are only cars, and "jam" names a distribution of them. As in Q-1, the facts are agreed and the dispute is about the word "thing." If having its own stable dynamics is enough, which I think it is, the jam qualifies. It adds no ingredient beyond cars and their spacing.

**Answer 2:**

A thing, specifically a process-thing. A phantom jam keeps its identity while every car in it changes. Cars enter at the back and leave at the front, and the jam itself often travels backward, against the flow. It has a speed, a size and a lifespan, and traffic-wave models (Lighthill-Whitham-Richards) treat it as a wave. That is the glider again: a persistent pattern moving through a substrate whose members keep changing.

## C63057

**Question:** Is a block in the Game of Life (a still life) more than its four cells?

**Answer 1:**

The block is four live cells in a 2×2 square with an empty margin around them. Nothing beyond that, but the arrangement and the margin both count.

The facts check out:
- Each live cell has exactly 3 live neighbours, so each survives.
- Each dead cell bordering an edge has 2 live neighbours.
- Each diagonal corner cell has 1.

No dead cell reaches 3, so nothing is born and the pattern holds. Being a "still life" is a property of the configuration, including the surrounding dead cells. It belongs to no single cell.

Unlike the glider, the block keeps the same four cells forever, so identifying it with those cells works well here. The alternative says the block simply is the four cells. That is defensible as long as "the four cells" includes their arrangement and empty surroundings. Whether that counts as "more" is a question of wording.

**Answer 2:**

Slightly more, and it makes a good contrast with Q-1. A block's stability is relational. Each live cell has exactly three live neighbors, so it survives, and no surrounding dead cell has exactly three, so nothing is born. Scatter the same four cells apart and they all die in one generation. Being a still life belongs to the arrangement.

Unlike the glider, the block keeps the same cells for its whole life, so pattern and material coincide over time. The block is the case where the "more" is pure structure. The glider is the case where the pattern also separates from its material. The block still has pattern-level behavior: in some glider collisions it acts as an eater, and in others it gets destroyed.

## C54118

**Question:** Is a pile of bricks anything more than the bricks?

**Answer 1:**

Barely. A pile is close to the zero case of "more than the parts." Rearrange the bricks and it's still a pile with nearly the same properties. What it adds is thin: a total mass, a rough shape, an angle of repose.

That makes it a useful control. Stack the same bricks into an arch and the arrangement carries load the bricks can't carry loose. How much "more" a whole is depends on how much work its organization does, and in a pile it does almost none.

**Answer 2:**

Close to simple. A pile is the bricks plus one modest fact: they are gathered together in one place. Beyond that, there is little more to it.

A good test is how sensitive the whole is to arrangement. Shuffle a pile and it is still a pile. Remove one brick and it is still a pile. Build the same bricks into an arch and the arrangement does real work: move one brick and the arch can fall. So a pile sits near the minimal end of composition, and it is a useful contrast with the glider, the jam, and the block.

The alternatives are universalism (any bricks form a genuine whole) and nihilism (there is no pile, only bricks arranged pile-wise). Both describe the pile identically, which suggests the dispute is about wording. The pile also has vague edges: how many bricks does it take to make one?

## C27112

**Question:** A character in a video game cannot find the graphics card, so the game world is less real than the hardware. Agree?

**Answer 1:**

Disagree. The argument moves from "can't detect the substrate" to "less real," and that step fails. The character's inability to find the GPU shows that the game level is causally closed: its regularities run on their own terms. We can't find our neurons by introspection either, and that makes our thoughts no less real.

What holds is asymmetric dependence: the game needs some hardware, and the hardware doesn't need the game. Dependence is a different claim from degree of reality. The game can also run on different hardware (multiple realizability), which makes it more independent of any particular substrate than the substrate is of it. By the argument's logic, chemistry would be less real than physics.

**Answer 2:**

Disagree. The argument as stated is invalid, though a nearby argument has some force.

The hidden assumption is that if X can't be detected from inside level L, X is more real than L. Checking that: the failure runs both ways. Inspecting transistors never turns up "the dragon" either. Each level's vocabulary lacks the other's terms, so failing to find something shows a difference of level and says nothing about rank.

The stronger argument is asymmetric dependence. The hardware can exist without the game, and the game world cannot exist without some hardware. Even this has a wrinkle: the same game runs on many different machines, so it depends on hardware in general and on no particular card. Getting from "depends on" to "less real" needs one more premise, that fundamentality comes in degrees and confers degrees of reality. Some metaphysicians accept that premise, and nothing in the question supplies it. My conclusion is that the game world is dependent, and calling it "less real" is a further, contestable step.

## C02711

**Question:** Is a car engine made of its parts?

**Answer 1:**

Elements: block, pistons, crankshaft, valves, and so on at one instant. Pattern: the four-stroke cycle that turns fuel into rotation. It persists through rebuilds. The two descriptions pick out different things. Same elements, no pattern: a stripped engine in labelled boxes has every part and no engine. Different elements, same pattern: an engine with new pistons, rings and bearings after ten years is the same engine on the service record.

"Made of" is a snapshot question. At any instant the engine is made of its parts, but only when they are arranged so the cycle can run. The arrangement is part of the answer.

Answer: yes, it is made of its parts in a specific working arrangement. What survives a rebuild is that arrangement and its cycle.

**Answer 2:**

Materially, yes. Every atom in the engine belongs to some part. The engine is those parts in a specific working arrangement, though. Disassembled parts in a crate weigh the same and produce no torque. Compression, timing and combustion belong to the arranged whole.

It also survives part replacement. Swap the spark plugs or a piston and you have the same engine, so the engine's identity follows the organization more than any particular part.

## C29265

**Question:** Is a river the same river from one year to the next?

**Answer 1:**

Yes, under the identity condition rivers actually have. A river is a persistent flow pattern through a basin and channel. The water changing is what being a river consists in. If the water stopped changing, you'd have a pond.

Identity here comes in degrees and has vague edges. Rivers meander, migrate, split, and get dammed into reservoirs, and at some point "same river" stops applying. Year to year, normally, it clearly applies.

**Answer 2:**

By the ordinary criteria for rivers, yes. By a same-matter criterion, no, and that criterion is the wrong one for rivers.

The concept "river" was built for flowing water, so turnover of the water is expected. River identity follows continuity of the channel, the source and outlet, the drainage basin, and the flow pattern. Heraclitus's point holds for the water and the moment. It doesn't settle the question for the river.

The alternative is that strict identity requires the same matter (mereological essentialism), on which nothing that metabolizes or flows persists. What decides it is which kind of thing you are tracking. The hard cases are real: when a river abruptly jumps to a new channel (avulsion), or is dammed into a lake, the ordinary criteria come apart and the answer becomes partly a matter of convention.
