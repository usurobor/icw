# Mind and brain

The case where "emerges from" carries the most weight. Terms are in the
[README](../README.md#two-ways-of-counting), credits in
[PRIOR-ART.md](../PRIOR-ART.md), and IDs point to
[`specs/ARTICULATION.md`](../specs/ARTICULATION.md).

## The computer first

A running computer can be followed as electrical activity or as software
executing. Each has its own criterion of sameness.

- **Circuit.** The same circuit, through any change in the computation it
  takes part in.
- **Variable.** The same variable, through any change in the circuits that
  hold it.

Witnesses in both directions:

- *Same circuit, different variables.* A memory cell holds one variable, is
  freed, then holds another.
- *Same variable, different circuits.* A variable moved to another address
  stays, for the program, one variable.

The candidate is `circuit < the running computer > variable`. **Verdict:** the
criteria differ and cross-cut (D7); carrying is undecided (O2):
**uncarried**, pending.

## Four sentences

**"A variable needs a circuit to hold it."** True: that is dependence. It
ranks neither criterion (R3). A circuit equally needs some matter to hold it,
and the matter carries on after the circuit is melted down. If needing a
holder made a criterion secondary, the ranking would run down to physics.

**"No transistor stores a variable."** True, and no transistor carries the
whole machine's electrical activity either. Both are whole-level. The same
holds in the brain: no neuron feels pain, and no neuron carries the brain's
activity.

**"Neural activity produces experience."** Cause and effect are two events.
If the pair were articulated, there would be one change read through two
criteria, and a causal claim across them would be a claim about that one
change (R5, proposed). It can be true. It names no second process.

**"Experience emerges."** Then experience is either a second event or the
same process under another criterion. A second event either does nothing the
neural activity has not done or gives every action two sufficient causes
(Kim 1998). The same process makes "emerges" a name for a whole-level
criterion, which the sentences above already grant.

## Where the brain case stops

The computer passes check 3. The brain case does not reach it yet. Neural
sameness is easy to state; experiential sameness, what makes this the same
pain a moment later, is not. Until it is stated, checks 2 and 3 cannot run.
Neuroscience gives a hint: learned behaviour stays stable over weeks while
the neurons that encode it change (Driscoll et al. 2017). Behaviour is not
experience, so the hint stays a hint.

The verdicts sort the rival views. Identity theory (Place 1956, Smart 1959)
needs the two criteria to coincide (D5). Emergence with a direction needs one
to nest in the other, and even then nesting ranks neither (R3).
Co-articulation needs them to cross-cut and both to be carried.

## The hard problem

Why there is experience at all (Chalmers 1995) becomes, in these terms: why
does this whole carry an experiential criterion? That is O2 for this case,
the theory's main open problem. ICW places the question and has no answer.

## Challenges

Searle (1990) holds that physics does not fix which program a machine runs.
Chalmers (1996) and Piccinini (2015) answer that physical causal structure
does. If they are right, the variable criterion may nest in the physical one,
and the computer case would end at **nested**.
